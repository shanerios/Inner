About

It started with listening.

My mother told me stories about unexplained things she had experienced. I listened. I don’t know what those experiences were, and I won’t claim to.

I grew up in a Catholic household. As I got older, I found I couldn’t fully reconcile the explanations I’d inherited with some of what I had seen and lived through. I won’t go into that here.

What my mother’s stories gave me was permission to consider that the explanations I’d been handed might not be the only ones.

So I started reading: about consciousness, lucid dreaming, out-of-body experiences, accounts of extraterrestrial phenomena, and what people say happens at the edges of sleep.

At some point reading wasn’t enough. I came across the Gateway Experience, a series of guided audio exercises from The Monroe Institute, and I wanted to experience these questions for myself, rather than simply decide what I believed.

I also remembered a lucid dream from when I was younger. Reading about lucid dreaming deepened my fascination, and I learned it’s something people study in sleep laboratories.

Sound kept appearing along the way: in ritual, in meditation, in how people settle themselves for sleep, in the way a voice or a rhythm can change how we feel. I began to wonder what role sound might play at the threshold of sleep, and inside dreaming itself.

I don’t tell this story because any of it settled anything. What it taught me was to keep questioning, and to go and look.

The question

If sound can shape experience while we’re awake, what might happen when it follows us into the dream?

We are listening before we are born. By the last months of pregnancy a baby can hear, and soon after birth it prefers its mother’s voice, long before it understands a word.

As adults, we know sound can change how we feel. In every society one large cross-cultural study sampled, people made music and tied it to things like infant care, healing, dance and love.

What happens when sound goes with us into sleep is much less clear. That is the question Inner asks. I don’t have the answer.

What we know — and what we don’t.

Inner is a practice for the threshold of sleep. It is also an inquiry, and this is territory that’s easy to overstate, so I try to keep four kinds of statement apart.

What we know

A sleeping brain still registers sound, at least at a basic level.

Sounds from outside sometimes find their way into dreams. How often, and why, is not settled.

Lucid dreaming, which means knowing you’re dreaming while it happens, has been verified in sleep laboratories. No method of inducing it has been shown to work reliably.

What people report

People describe awareness that seems to move outside the body, often near the edge of sleep, sometimes with buzzing or vibrating sensations. What it is remains debated; spiritual, psychological and neurological explanations all exist.

You don’t have to accept these experiences as proof of anything. You don’t have to dismiss them, either.

What we’re beginning to learn

Early studies suggest that sounds paired with training before sleep can raise the chance of a lucid dream in some people. The effect appears to come from the link a person learns between the sound and a way of paying attention, not from the sound alone.

None of this yet tells us how reliably sound can shape a dream.

What Inner explores

Inner is a set of practices and systems I’m exploring. It is not a claim about what reality is.

The Chambers are guided sound experiences meant to be returned to.

Recognition cues are tones at set moments across the night that ask a simple question: could this be a dream? They are in the spirit of the early research above. They are not proof of it, and I don’t claim they make anyone lucid.

Procedural nights are overnight soundscapes built in stages from a few choices you make.

The Guardians and the Witnesses are frameworks I created for working with awareness near sleep. They are Inner’s own, not established findings.

Inner creates conditions for exploring these questions. It doesn’t tell anyone what their experience means. Spiritual, symbolic, psychological and phenomenological readings are all welcome here, and none is required. It does not promise lucid dreams, out-of-body experiences or medical outcomes, and it is not a substitute for healthy sleep or professional care. Protect your rest first.

What I’d like to find out

Does preparation matter, and what kind? Does it matter when a sound arrives, how often, how gently? Does the room, the hour, or the way a night is built change anything? Why might one person respond to a cue that another never notices?

Underneath those questions is one I keep returning to:

Under what conditions can waking intention survive the transition into dreaming?

Someday I’d like Inner to be able to learn from experiences people deliberately choose to share: how they prepared, what they heard and when, what they reported. If patterns exist, perhaps nights could be built to fit a person better. If they don’t, that would be worth knowing too.

Inner doesn’t do this today. It doesn’t compare experiences across people, and it doesn’t know what helps you become lucid. This is inquiry, not published research.

We’re not there yet. And I don’t know what we’ll find.

Who makes Inner

I’m Shane Rios. I voice Inner, and collaborators and sound designers have contributed to the audio.

Auditory Ascension is where I think through sound, perception and consciousness, and why sound might matter at the edge of sleep. Guardians is another framework that grew out of the same inquiry. The books explore the ideas; Inner is where I try to put them into practice.
